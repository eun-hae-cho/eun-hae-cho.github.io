Using assessment to guide instruction.

My teaching connects academic communication, special education, and professional preparation. I design learning that moves from supported participation toward increasingly independent, evidence-informed judgment.

Graduate coursework · EDTL 3103 · Fall 2026

My coursework in Assessment, Instructional Planning, and Practice develops my ability to connect assessment evidence with instructional decisions. I am examining how brief measures, error analysis, and repeated progress monitoring can guide support while accounting for learners’ opportunities to learn, language backgrounds, and access needs.

This work complements my preparation in special education and behavior analysis by asking what a score can tell us, what additional information is needed, and how assessment should inform the next instructional step.

Choosing evidence for the decision

Assessment begins with a question about learning. I distinguish curriculum-based assessments of taught content from curriculum-based measures used to sample skills and monitor progress. Screening, instructional planning, progress monitoring, and diagnostic assessment serve different purposes.

From scoring to interpretation

Early literacy, numeracy, and mathematics examples help me examine administration and scoring choices. Number identification, quantity discrimination, and missing-number tasks sample different skills. Reading examples add letter naming and nonsense-word fluency. Interpretation requires distinguishing rate from accuracy, comparing progress with goals, and following each measure’s scoring rules.

Connecting assessment with support

Within multi-tiered systems of support (MTSS), assessment informs instructional intensity. Data-based individualization connects progress monitoring, error analysis, instructional adaptation, and further monitoring. Powell and Stecker (2014) illustrate this iterative process in mathematics intervention.

I am also developing ways to explain results clearly to families, including why an instructional-level measure may be useful without describing a learner as fixed at a particular grade or developmental level.

What I am learning

Accurate measurement is necessary, but its value depends on the decisions it supports. I want to interpret patterns across observations, consider the conditions under which learners perform, and communicate both the usefulness and the limits of the evidence.
